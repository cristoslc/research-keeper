# Faceted Classification and Faceted Taxonomies

by Heather Hedden -- February 24, 2024

Modern taxonomies that are used to support information management and findability are more similar to information retrieval thesauri and subject heading schemes than they are to classification systems. The method of "faceted classification," however, does apply to types of taxonomies.

## What is faceted classification?

Facet means face, side, dimension, or aspect. Facets are meant to mean aspects of classification. A digital content item has multiple informational dimensions or aspects to it and thus multiple ways to be classified.

Classification is about putting an item into a class or category. In faceted classification, an item cannot physically be in more than one place, but it can still be "assigned to" more than one class. So, while the book itself can be on only one shelf, the record *about* the book can be assigned to more than one class.

Faceted classification assigns classes/categories/terms/concepts from each of multiple facets to a content item, allowing users to find the item by choosing the concepts from any one of the facets they consider first. Different users will consider different classification facets first. Users then narrow the search results by selecting concepts from additional facets in any order they wish, until they get a targeted result set meeting the criteria of multiple facet selections.

## History of faceted classification

The first faceted classification was developed and published by mathematician/librarian S.R. Ranganathan in 1933, as an alternative to the Dewey Decimal System for classifying books, called Colon Classification. Its facets:

1. *Personality* -- topic or orientation
2. *Matter* -- things or materials
3. *Energy* -- actions
4. *Space* -- places or locations
5. *Time* -- times or time periods

In the late 20th century, digital library research systems based on databases enabled faceted classification and search, with different fields of a database record represented in different search facets. Faceted classification and browsing gained widespread adoption with the advancement of interactive user interfaces on websites and in web applications in the late 1990s and early 2000s.

## Structure of facets

Another way to look at faceted classification is to consider a facet for each of various question words: What, Who, Where, When:

- *What* kind of thing is it -- content type
- *What* is it primarily about -- subject
- *Who* is it for or concerns -- audience or user group
- *Where* is it for/applicable -- geographic region
- *When* it is about -- event or season

A logical and practical number of facets tends to be in the range of three to seven.

A standard feature of facets is that they are mutually exclusive. A concept/type belongs to only one facet. The difference is that in *faceted* classification it is merely the concept that belongs to just one facet, not the content item itself.

## When a faceted taxonomy is not for classification

The design, implementation and use of facets to construct or refine searches has become so popular that it is no longer used just for classification aspects. Faceted classification is intended to classify things that share all the same facets. But with more heterogeneous content, facets are not universally shared.

### Facets for organizing subjects

In faceted classification we assign an object or content item to multiple different classes relevant to the content item as a whole. This contrasts with indexing or tagging for subjects or names that occur *within* a text. These can be grouped into facets for filtering without being about "classification" of the item.

Examples of specific subject-type facets: Organization types, Product types, Technologies, Activities, Industries, Disciplines, Job roles, Event types, Topics.

The "Topics" facet is then used for the leftover generic subject concepts that do not belong in any of the other specialized facets. Unlike faceted classification, each facet is applicable to only some content items.

### Facets for organizing controlled vocabularies

Faceted filters may be based on different controlled vocabulary types: term lists, name authorities, and subject thesauri/taxonomies. Facets could be used for: Named people, Organizations, Products/brands, Divisions/departments, Named works/document titles, Places, Topics.

The Topics facet could contain a large hierarchical taxonomy or thesaurus. Thus, there is even a nuanced difference between a faceted browse UI that utilizes a taxonomy and a "faceted taxonomy."

## When faceted classification is not a taxonomy

Taxonomies are primarily for subjects and may include named entities. There exists additional metadata for filtering that is out of scope of taxonomy: date published, file format, author, document status, etc.

Attributes are a form of faceted classification, but a set of attributes is not really a faceted taxonomy. Ecommerce taxonomies tend to be hierarchical (categories/subcategories of product types), with attributes at lower levels for narrowing: color, size, price range, and product-specific features.

## Conclusions

Not all faceted taxonomies are faceted classifications, but some are. Not all faceted classifications are taxonomies, but some are. The differences are nuanced, and end-users don't care about these distinctions, as long as the taxonomist does. Having a deep understanding of facets helps taxonomists design the facets better. The goal is to serve the users with the most suitable faceted design to accommodate the set of content.
