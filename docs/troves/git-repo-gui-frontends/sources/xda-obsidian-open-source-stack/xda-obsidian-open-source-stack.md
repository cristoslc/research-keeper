# I've replaced my entire productivity stack with open-source tools, except for Obsidian

By Nolen Jonker, XDA Developers, December 5, 2025

I've slowly been swapping out many of my daily tools for open-source alternatives. It's a combination of feeling a little more secure about not relying on the cloud, plus open-source apps are also getting really cool — they're not as outdated as was once the case. My most-used open-source apps at the moment are AFFiNE, a project management app, and Penpot, a prototyping design tool. And I can't really imagine going back to their proprietary counterparts.

But the one closed-source app I haven't replaced is Obsidian. It's not open-source, but it is local-first and hits almost all of the checkboxes that I look for in a notes app and personal knowledge management tool. Not many of the Obsidian open-source alternatives I've tried hit the same mix of speed, extensions, and day-to-day usability. So Obsidian is one of the few proprietary apps that survived my transition to open-source options.

## Why isn't Obsidian open-source?

Obsidian isn't open-source because the development team prefers to focus on development over open-source maintenance. It's also a business decision — closed-source models tend to be more attractive to investors. According to one of the co-founders, open-source would add a lot of overhead, like code reviews and community forks, which drain time and resources away from feature development.

Monetization also plays a role. While the core of Obsidian is free for personal use, there are optional paid services and licenses for things like sync and publishing. Making it fully open would interfere with that revenue model.

That said, Obsidian does give us some of the same benefits people expect from open-source. For starters, it's local-first by default, so all your notes live on your device unless you specify otherwise, and the app also doesn't collect personal information. Obsidian is fundamentally a Markdown editor, which is a universal and open format, so you're not locked into anything proprietary and your data remains portable, easy to back up, and future-proof.

Moreover, much of Obsidian's plugin ecosystem is open-source, and anyone can build, share, and tweak those plugins. So while Obsidian isn't open-source, you get a similar privacy-focused, community-driven, and customized experience as you would with open-source.

## Why I've kept Obsidian

I've used Obsidian on and off for a while now. And even though I'm not a power user or have complex setups, it has a bunch of features that make it worth keeping in my stack. For starters, Markdown support is solid — I can write simple notes quickly and format them however I want. Plus I can link between all my notes without worrying about weird file types or formats. Speaking of, linking is also seamless. All it takes is using the double bracket function to create a whole backlinking system in one document. Furthermore, the Graph gives me a nice visual overview of those links and a way to quickly jump between them. This is honestly where I get the most use out of Obsidian — notes and links.

But the Canvas is probably my favorite part of the app. It not only lets me brainstorm, build mind maps, and see the visual presentation of my work, but I can also upload media files and interactive web pages. This lets me build a sort of media hub with both local and web files. It pretty much rules out every other diagramming app in my arsenal.

Lately, I've brought Perplexity into my note-taking stack, and I'm relying on Obsidian to do it. I export my Perplexity threads as Markdown files straight into my Obsidian vault, from where I can view them with the formatting and web links intact, as well as edit and integrate them with the rest of my notes. And last but not least, Obsidian now has Bases, which is basically a simplified version of databases. It lets me use advanced filters to sort through my documents and folders.

## Obsidian still fits in an open-source workflow

Obsidian still works really well in an open-source-leaning stack or workflow. Most of that comes from the way it handles files. Everything lives in plain Markdown in a regular folder on your device, so I can move the documents around or move them between devices with Syncthing. For a closed-source app, that's about as open as it gets.

It also plays nicely with the other productivity and notes apps I use. I can easily move my notes between Obsidian and Joplin. Logseq and AFFiNE support those Markdown files, too. I can even embed my SVG design files in Obsidian straight from Penpot.

### The proprietary app I'm keeping

Even though Obsidian isn't open-source, it still earns its spot in the rest of my open-source toolkit just because of how it functions. Everything I use it for is free — most people will never have to pay for a license for daily personal use. It uses Markdown, plays well with my other open-source apps, keeps everything local, and doesn't gobble my personal data. I don't really feel the need to ditch it just because it's not open-source.
