# Encoding the Citation Life-Cycle

Gou, Meng, Chinchilla-Rodríguez, Bu (2022). Scientometrics 127, 5027–5052.

## The four-period citation life-cycle model

1. **Sleeping period** (Period I): Publication receives no or extremely few citations. Duration varies by discipline: Arts & Humanities papers may sleep for 5+ years; Physics papers typically wake within 1 year.
2. **Recognition period** (Period II): Citations grow rapidly as the work is discovered. On average 5 years for Arts & Humanities papers published in 1985.
3. **Citing period** (Period III): Sustained high citations. This is the "active life" of the publication.
4. **Death period** (Period IV): Citations decline to near-zero. The paper is effectively obsolete.

## Literature revival

Some publications exhibit **multiple citation life-cycles**: they go through sleeping→recognition→citing→death, and then experience a revival where citations grow again. This contradicts the assumption that obsolescence is monotonic.

## Disciplinary differences in sleeping period

Arts & Humanities: ~5 years sleeping, then rapid recognition.
Physics: nearly immediate recognition (no meaningful sleeping period).
Social sciences: intermediate, ~2-3 years.

## Implications for rk

- The sleeping period means some sources should not be staled on publication date alone. A source that hasn't been cited much may be sleeping, not obsolete.
- Literature revival means the `rk recall` operation (bringing a forgotten source back to fresh) must be cheap and easy, because "forgotten" is not necessarily "forever."
- The death period is the bibliometric analogue of `forgettable` — citations have dropped to near-zero, and the work is unlikely to be cited again without an external event triggering revival.
- The `rk dream` contradiction check is essentially a diachronous analysis: it asks whether the current knowledge state still supports the source's claims, which is what distinguishes genuine death from a sleeping period.
