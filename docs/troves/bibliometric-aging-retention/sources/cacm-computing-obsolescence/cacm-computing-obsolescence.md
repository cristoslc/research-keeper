# Confronting the Myth of Rapid Obsolescence in Computing Research

Larson (2007), Communications of the ACM.

## Key findings

Computing's citing half-life was measured at **7.5 years** — in striking contrast to the only prior study (Cunningham and Bocock), which found a citing half-life of **4 years** for CS.

The Price Index (proportion of references that are ≤5 years old) for computing was comparable to physics (~40%), suggesting CS is not uniquely fast-obsolescing compared to other hard sciences.

## The myth of rapid CS obsolescence

The common belief that computing literature becomes obsolete quickly is not supported by citation data. Reviewers frequently mention "old references" as a criticism, but the data shows computing's half-life is comparable to other established sciences.

The median citation age of scientific publications has been **increasing steadily since the 1970s**, likely due to the availability of online bibliographic databases making it easier to access and cite older references.

## Implications for rk

- Programming framework sources may indeed have short half-lives (1-2 years), but foundational CS concepts have half-lives comparable to physics (7-8+ years)
- The distinction between "frontier CS" (fast-obsolescing) and "core CS" (stable) mirrors the frontier/core distinction from Cole's work
- A topic-level TTL for "software engineering" should not assume the same obsolescence rate as "current-events tech news"
