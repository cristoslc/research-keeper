# Progress in Science — Comte's Hierarchy and Core vs Frontier

## Comte's hierarchy

Auguste Comte (19th century) argued there is a hierarchy of sciences: astronomy → physics → chemistry → biology → sociology. Sciences atop the hierarchy have:

- More highly developed theories
- Greater use of mathematical language
- Higher levels of consensus on theory, methods, and significance
- More use of theory to make verifiable predictions
- **Faster obsolescence of research** — citations drop off rapidly over time
- Relatively fast progress

Sciences at the bottom exhibit the opposite characteristics.

## Core vs frontier distinction (Cole, 1983, 1992)

Key finding: different fields have similar features at the **frontier** but very different features at the **core**.

- At the frontier (proposal review, active research): consensus about quality is NOT systematically higher in natural sciences than social sciences. Standard deviations of reviewer ratings were twice as large in meteorology as in economics.
- At the core (undergraduate textbooks): dramatic differences. Physics and chemistry textbook references had median publication dates before 1900. Sociology references: post-1960. Physics/chemistry cited ~100 references; sociology ~800.
- Comparing 1950s and 1970s texts: physics and chemistry covered much the same material and cited the same sources. Sociology's newer texts cited only a small proportion of the sources in earlier texts.

**Implication**: core knowledge in physics/chemistry is both more consensual and more stable over time than core knowledge in sociology. This provides a direct empirical basis for per-domain TTL classification.

## Progress is nonlinear

- Some ideas lead to rapid revolutions; others have lengthy gestation or protracted resistance.
- Some achieve overly rapid faddish acceptance followed by quick dismissal.
- The majority of scientific products have limited impact; only a few become seminal.
- Whether new ideas survive depends on environmental factors — a selective struggle.

## Societal contribution lag

"It takes an average of 17 years for research evidence to reach clinical practice" (Balas and Boren, 2000). This suggests a delay between frontier knowledge and durable core knowledge — the consolidation window.
